Your product doesn't need an ad. It needs a format.

What a famous curry taught me about creators with no budget.

Brick Lane Curry House has a dish with a reputation. Its phaal is the curry Adam Richman took on in Man v. Food, and people still walk in asking for it by name. That's the kind of story most brands would pay for. What the restaurant didn't have was a budget for paid creators.

So we stopped treating the phaal like a menu item and started treating it like a format.

The villain has a backstory

The phaal started as the least interesting dish on the menu. Chef Satinder Sharma, who opened Brick Lane on East 6th Street in 2002, decided that if he was going to make something spicy, he'd do it justice. Habaneros first, then Scotch bonnets, bhut jolokia and the Carolina Reaper, bought straight from Ed Currie, the grower who bred it.

When Man v. Food showed up, the dish "snowballed into something so big, unintentionally," he told me. He calls it the infamous phaal who made them famous. A story like that is already doing the work an ad would do. It just needs somewhere to happen every week.

A challenge is already a show

Look at what the phaal challenge already has: a known villain, a clear stake and a reaction at the end. That isn't an ad. It's a show that can run every week. Formats are what bring people back, because the audience knows the shape and wants to see who's next.

So we turned the challenge into a recurring content property on the restaurant's own channels. One phaal reel, a collaboration with First We Feast, reached 1.47 million views.

Creators are contestants, not billboards

A format also fixes the budget problem. If the phaal is a show, creators aren't people you pay to hold your food up to the camera. They're contestants, and they want the content as much as the restaurant wants the reach.

I pitched more than ten food creators myself, DMs and all, on a zero-budget creator strategy. We brought in Priya's Plates as our guest and posted her attempt as a collaboration, so it reached both audiences at once. It passed 58,000 views with nearly 300 shares.

How to find your format

Most brands already have one and walk past it. Look for the thing customers repeat about you without being asked: the dish people dare each other to order, the question every new client asks, the ritual your regulars have.

Then give it a shape that repeats. The same setup, the same stakes, a new face every time. Run it on your own channels so you own it, and invite creators in as guests so their audiences meet you inside a story instead of an ad break.

If your product has a famous story, it doesn't need an ad. It needs a format.
