What happened when we took Canvas to SF and LA Tech Week.

Two events in three days, an Oscar-winning filmmaker on the bill, and the trailers for my series in front of both rooms.

In October, Canvas went west for Tech Week: SF Tech Week ran October 5 to 11 and LA Tech Week ran October 12 to 18. I went as Canvas's creative director, with Canvas CEO Abhinav Singla. We hosted two events, went to plenty more, and pitched Canvas at every one.

This is what the week looked like, why a micro drama studio belongs at a tech week at all, and what I'd tell any small team about pitching one slate to rooms that want different things.

San Francisco: the Canvas launch party.

On Saturday, October 10, Anwar Events hosted the Canvas Launch Party: Creators Are the New Studios at 555 Grant Avenue. Ilias Anwar moderated a panel with Abhinav, Ben Woollams of TrueRights and Pinto Labs CTO Xavier Santos. 371 guests were on the list.

I played the trailers for the series I directed, and the slate went up in four lanes: fashion, travel, fiction and non-fiction. The Red Carpet Show, our 90-second red carpet interview series with Ilias, was there too.

Los Angeles: The Hybrid Studio.

Two days later, on Monday, October 12, House of 434 hosted The Hybrid Studio: From Oscar-Winning Films to AI-Native Studios at 11520 West Pico Boulevard, listed as an Andreessen Horowitz event and in dot.LA's guide to Monday at LA Tech Week. Shilpi Nayak opened. Hemal Trivedi, co-founder of ZeroInfinity, presented, and so did Abhinav. Hemal edited Saving Face, the documentary that won the Oscar for Best Documentary Short, and won an Emmy for that edit. Then she and Abhinav sat down for a panel on where craft meets AI, with an investor in the AI video company Higgsfield, and the night closed with an open Q and A.

Around 300 people came. I played our trailers again, and the conversations after were the good kind: filmmakers, AI filmmakers and investors in one room, all asking what a small team can make now.

Why micro dramas belong at a tech week.

Micro dramas are a phone business before they're a film business. Sensor Tower counted $2.98 billion in in-app revenue for micro and short drama apps in 2025, up 115% on the year before, and time spent in those apps grew by 5.78 billion hours. In the first quarter of 2025, the US alone brought in 49% of global revenue. And those apps sent 68% of their US ad spending to social networks from January to September 2025, which tells you where audiences get found.

The creator side is just as big. Goldman Sachs Research estimated the creator economy at $250 billion in 2023 and projected $480 billion by 2027. Canvas sits where the two meet: series built around creators who already have an audience, made for the phone. That's a tech story as much as a film story, so we took it to the rooms where the tech money and the AI tools are.

What the week was really for.

Hosting was the visible part. The rest of the week, Abhinav and I pitched every day, at every event, to investors, filmmakers and AI filmmakers. In between, we shot about four series across both cities, filming while we hosted and pitched. We also spent a couple of hours talking film with director Jeremiah S. Chechik, who made National Lampoon's Christmas Vacation and Benny and Joon.

The pitch fits on a hoodie: creators are the new studios. Our slate is the proof. Face Value, Red Flags, Taser Fight Championship, Dear NYC and Demo Day are a few of the more than twelve series I've directed for Canvas.

How to pitch one slate to two different rooms.

Host, don't just attend. We partnered with hosts who already had the room: Anwar Events in San Francisco and House of 434 in Los Angeles. Their lists brought the people; we brought the work.

Show the work, not a deck. I played the trailers in both rooms. A two-minute episode explains the format faster than any slide.

Sort the slate into lanes. In SF it went up in four: fashion, travel, fiction and non-fiction, so everyone could find the one that was theirs.

Change the door, not the house. San Francisco was billed as Creators Are the New Studios. Los Angeles was The Hybrid Studio, about Oscar-winning craft meeting AI-native studios. Same series, a different way in.

Pitch every day. Hosting took two nights. Every other day was pitching at whatever event we were at.

Make something while you're there. We shot about four series across both cities, and came home with an after-movie for each event.

Cutting for a loud room.

Every Canvas episode is under two minutes and built on hooks and cliffhangers, and a trailer for a loud room needs the same build. Open on the hook, so the first seconds earn the next ones. Put the creator on screen early, because their face is the reason a stranger already cares. Close on a question, not a logo. In a room full of conversations, nobody waits for a slow build.

The after-movies work on the same clock. Each one runs about 40 seconds, and they do the job the event can't do on its own: keep working after everyone goes home.

A checklist for a small team at a big tech week.

Pick two or three events your audience already goes to, and find a host who already has the room.

Cut trailers that still work with the sound off. Event rooms are loud.

Write one line that fits on a hoodie. Ours: creators are the new studios.

Make a one-page slate: every title, its creator and its lane.

Bring a camera, even a small crew. The after-movie keeps working long after the week ends.

Book meetings before you land. Calendars fill up fast during the week.

Follow up within two days, with the trailer link in the first line.

Mistakes to avoid.

Bringing a deck and no footage.

Giving every room the same pitch. Keep the slate, change the way in.

Spending the whole week at panels and none of it shooting.

Treating the event as the finish line. The follow-up is the work.

Forgetting to photograph your own team. The pictures of you in the room are the ones you'll use for months.
