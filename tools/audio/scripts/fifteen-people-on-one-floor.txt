I joined W.L.D.D. when it was fifteen people on one floor.

From a top floor in Bangalore to owning ScoopWhoop: what a meme agency's early team taught me.

I joined W.L.D.D. in Bangalore in January 2023, when the whole company fit on the top floor of one building. There were about fifteen of us, and I was one of the first people in that office. I started as a junior brand strategist. Two months later I was a creative strategist, working on campaigns for brands I had grown up with.

Memes were the product.

W.L.D.D.'s edge was meme marketing: funny, quirky, shareable copy that people passed on because it was good, not because it was an ad. We did it for Zomato, Swiggy One, Zepto and Bingo! Mad Angles. For films, an organic meme push could put people in seats, and the audience never knew the joke they were sharing had been planned.

The engine was distribution. W.L.D.D. worked with hundreds of India's biggest meme pages, so one idea could land everywhere on the same day. Releases for Prime Video, Netflix India, ZEE5, Sony Pictures, Sony Music and South Indian cinema all ran through that network. By afaqs' count, the agency has worked with more than 120 brands on over 1,050 campaigns, with a community of more than 10,000 creators.

What I made there.

I pitched and closed Sony Pictures as W.L.D.D.'s first international client. Our Pope's Exorcist cinema stunt was covered by Hindustan Times, India Today and The Indian Express.

I worked on Thank You Durex, a fan page we built for Durex India. One reel passed 20.9 million views, and I'm the last person in it.

I ran W.L.D.D.'s own Instagram. A typical reel went from about 4,000 views to about 19,000.

I worked on ZEE5's Taj: Divided by Blood, Tu Jhoothi Main Makkaar and Kannada cinema, including PR and marketing around Yash and KGF: Chapter 2.

Then the building filled up.

Every few months we needed more room. We took the second floor, then the first, then the whole building. Then came a second building, for LIT School.

I left in 2024, at the peak of my time there, to study filmmaking in New York. W.L.D.D. kept growing. In February 2025 it bought ScoopWhoop from The Good Glamm Group in a deal reported at 20 crore rupees. A month later it took a majority stake in the sneaker brand 7-10, and in June 2025 it acquired the creative studio Imagined.

By July 2026 it had about 300 people across Bengaluru, Mumbai and Delhi. In September 2026, Adfactors PR and Vikas Khemani bought a stake, and Adfactors PR's CEO joined the board.

What I took with me.

That floor taught me three things I still use at Thava Studio in New York. Write it so people want to send it to a friend. Put it where the audience already is, not where you wish they were. And ship it while people are still talking about the thing it's about.

Rule one: write it so people want to send it.

The test I use: before anything goes out, name the friend you'd send it to. If you can't, it isn't ready.

Most sends come with a short message attached: this is so you, this is us, remember this? Write for that message. In my experience, a post that gives someone a reason to send it to one specific friend travels further than one that's merely good.

The platforms now measure exactly this. Instagram's head, Adam Mosseri, has said the three signals that matter most in ranking are watch time, likes and sends, and that sends matter a little more for reaching people who don't follow you yet. The research on why people share goes back further. Jonah Berger and Katherine Milkman studied three months of New York Times articles and found that positive content was shared more than negative, and that content stirring high-arousal feelings like awe, anger or anxiety spread further, while content that made people sad spread less.

Rule two: put it where the audience already is.

W.L.D.D. didn't wait for people to find a brand's page. It worked with hundreds of meme pages, so one idea could land everywhere on the same day. Sometimes that meant building a new home for the content altogether, as you'll see below.

For a brand today, that means asking where your people already spend their evenings: a creator's page, a meme page, a group chat, a community. Go there with something worth sending.

Rule three: ship it while people are still talking.

A joke about last week's news is a joke nobody sends. A small team has the edge here, because fewer people need to say yes. On that top floor, the distance between an idea and a post was a few desks.

Speed only works with taste, though. The 2025 Sprout Social Index, a survey of 4,044 consumers in the US, UK, Canada and Australia, found that a third of them think it's embarrassing when brands jump on viral trends. Fast and off-brand is worse than late.

What the rules looked like in practice.

Write it to be sent: for The Pope's Exorcist, we put a possessed woman in a movie theater as the trailer played. It was built to be filmed and passed on, and it reached millions of people across India.

Go where the audience already is: Thank You Durex was a fan page we built so the memes had a home of their own, instead of running on Durex India's premium page. One reel passed 20.9 million views.

Ship it while it's live: on W.L.D.D.'s own Instagram we turned agency news, festivals and events into reels, about one every five days, and wrote every caption to be sent to a friend. In 2022, 8 of 82 reels passed 10,000 views. In my sixteen months, 56 of 100 did.

A checklist before you post.

Can you name the friend you'd send it to?

Does it land in the first second, without the caption?

Does the brand belong in the joke, or was it stuck on at the end?

Is the moment still alive? If people have stopped talking about it, skip it.

Is it going where the audience already is, not only on your own page?

Mistakes to avoid.

Making an ad and calling it a meme. People can tell, and they don't send ads.

Chasing every trend. Take only the ones you can move into your brand's world.

Counting likes and ignoring sends. Likes say people enjoyed it; sends say it will travel.

Letting approvals kill the timing. Agree on taste up front, so the decision on the day is quick.

Posting only on your own page and calling it distribution.

Fifteen desks on a top floor became a company of about 300 people that owns ScoopWhoop. I'm glad I was there when it was small enough that every idea I pitched might get made.

When a team is small enough that every idea can ship, speed and taste beat budget.
