Hi, I'm Nived, and this is Unscripted.

So, how do I make a film in New York on an indie budget? Let me tell you how it started. I was at the New York Film Academy, in the one-year conservatory, directing, and we were shooting all the time, right? Five, six days a week, and weekends were for shoots. And very quickly I realized I can't spend what my classmates are spending. I come from a middle-class family, so I did what we do back home. We call it jugaad. You find the smart way, the cheap way, and you get it done.

And for me, jugaad was people. I made friends with everyone: actors, musical theater kids, cinematographers, anyone in the building. I went to every meetup. So when I had a story to make, I already knew who was right for it. I just called them. That's it. That's the whole trick. Obviously, they'll want pizza on set, but that's how it works.

Call your friends and family first. You don't have to hire every role off Backstage or pay for an assistant director and a D.P. on day one. Your friends might be better, and every shoot turns them into a team that's there for the next one.

You can see it in my credits. Nathan Flores is in The Last Pizza, Agent X and Before We Leave. Yi Chan, who goes by Ethan, acted in The Last Pizza, shot Before We Leave and was my assistant director on Agent X. Cito shot The Last Pizza and ran sound on Agent X. Same family, different films.

Today, if you asked me for a crew, I could put one together in seconds: a D.P., an assistant director, actors, sound, a colorist, an editor. That's the real trick. It's harder if you're shy, I get it. Do it anyway.

Micro dramas: the same family, a bigger slate.

The same approach carried into the micro dramas I direct for Canvas: more than twelve series so far, including Face Value, Red Flags, Dear NYC, Taser Fight Championship and Demo Day. Each runs four to ten episodes, every episode under two minutes. The constant on every one: me directing and Abhinav Singla on production.

Around us, the same people keep coming back. Nishita was my assistant director on Dear NYC. Navya did art on Dear NYC and Red Flags. Karun shot Dear NYC, and Kais Vasaya shot most of the others, including Face Value and Demo Day. Onil Shah colored most of them and shot some too.

Actors I met making short films, like Dillon Cain from Agent X and Cassidy, came back for the micro dramas. Post is friends too. Asla, who edits for my marketing studio, has cut micro drama promos, trailers, teasers and some full episodes. Adarsh has edited with me and Advait has helped. Some of the non-fiction series were edited by friends from my W.L.D.D. days: Madhur, Karthik and Tushar. And Akhand, my graphic designer from my marketing work, made most of the series posters.

They came from earlier sets, film school, my old office and cold DMs, not from a budget line.

Cold DMs work.

Join every community you can. I'm in New York filmmaker groups on WhatsApp and Facebook, and I meet people on Instagram all the time. When someone's work catches your eye, message them. Don't overthink what they'll think of you. A lot of my cinematographers, colorists and editors started as a cold DM on Instagram, and those friends introduced me to more.

New York is the set.

My films have cost under seven hundred dollars, and some cost nothing. Locations come from friends too: their apartments become your sets. Outside, New York does a lot of the work. It's backlit almost everywhere you point a camera, and backlight is free production value.

Manhattan streets, the Brooklyn Bridge, Bryant Park, Times Square, Central Park: take a camera and go. I rewrite scenes to fit the places I can get. Subways are easy to shoot, so sometimes the scene just moves to the subway.

On permits: the city's film office says you generally don't need one if you're shooting with a handheld camera or a camera on a tripod, with hand-held gear, and you aren't taking over city property or using vehicles, prop weapons, stunts or actors in police uniforms. Check the rules before you shoot. The subway belongs to the MTA, which has its own filming rules.

Pick stories your budget can carry.

Sci-fi and action eat money. I make realistic films: people talking, drama, comedy, romance, emotions, sometimes close to non-fiction. It's the work I love, and it happens to be the work an indie budget can carry. That's why making indie films came easy to me. It's just who I am as a filmmaker.

You're in good company.

None of this is new. Spike Lee shot She's Gotta Have It in Brooklyn in twelve days in the summer of 1985, for $175,000, with a non-union crew.

Over in New Jersey, Kevin Smith made Clerks for $27,575 in the convenience store where he worked, mostly on maxed-out credit cards. His longtime friend Walt Flanagan played four different customers.

And the micro drama business runs on the same math. A 100-page micro drama script can be shot in about a week, against a month for a feature, according to the Los Angeles Times, and whole seasons have been made for $50,000 to $250,000 with affordable, often non-union casts, according to Final Draft. Ours are shorter, and we lean even harder on the family.

Feed your crew.

Be kind to everyone on set, and feed them. Real food, not only pizza. People remember how you treated them, and they'll come back any day you call.

My budget was never money. It was people.
