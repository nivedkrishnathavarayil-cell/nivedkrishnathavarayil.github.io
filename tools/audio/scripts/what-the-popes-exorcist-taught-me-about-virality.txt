What the Pope's Exorcist taught me about virality.

And why most brands get it wrong.

In early 2023, I was a Creative Strategist at W.L.D.D., a scrappy startup of fifteen people sharing one office in Bangalore, India. We had big clients, bigger ambitions, and the kind of energy that only exists when a company is small enough that every idea you pitch might actually get made.

Sony Pictures India came to us with a brief for The Pope's Exorcist, a Hollywood horror film starring Russell Crowe, releasing in Indian theatres. The ask was straightforward: generate buzz, drive awareness, get people talking.

What happened next was covered by Hindustan Times and The Indian Express, reached millions of people across India, and earned W.L.D.D. its first international Hollywood client. W.L.D.D. has since grown from fifteen people to over a thousand, acquired ScoopWhoop and LIT School, and is now one of India's largest marketing agencies.

I'm not telling you this to brag. I'm telling you this because everything I learned about virality, real virality and not the manufactured kind, I learned from that campaign. And most of it runs counter to what brands are told to do.

1. Virality is not about the brand. It's about the moment.

The biggest mistake I see brands make is putting themselves at the centre of everything they create. Their logo, their tagline, their product shot, front and centre, every single time. The audience doesn't care. The audience cares about themselves, their culture, their emotions, and what's happening in their world right now.

The Pope's Exorcist campaign worked because we didn't lead with the film. We led with the audience's experience of watching a horror trailer in a theatre: the anticipation, the darkness, the collective held breath of a crowd that's about to be scared. We understood that moment viscerally. And we built an idea that hijacked it in a way that felt completely organic.

When people shared the content, they weren't sharing a movie promotion. They were sharing something that made them feel something. That's the difference.

2. Cultural fluency beats production value every single time.

We didn't have a massive budget. We didn't have a celebrity. We didn't have a production crew or a fancy shoot. What we had was a deep understanding of how Indian audiences engage with horror, with cinema, with meme culture, and with the specific pleasure of being scared in a crowded theatre with strangers.

That cultural fluency, knowing what would make this specific audience stop scrolling, lean in and send it to their friends, was worth more than any production budget. I grew up watching films the way Indian audiences watch films: loudly, communally, emotionally. I knew what would land because I was the audience.

This is why I'm always sceptical of global brands that create one piece of content and try to push it across every market. Virality is local before it's global. It spreads because it feels like it was made for you specifically, not for everyone vaguely.

3. The idea has to be willing to be uncomfortable.

Safe content does not go viral. I have never seen a perfectly polished, brand-approved, legal-reviewed, twelve-rounds-of-feedback piece of content genuinely break through. Not once. The content that travels is always the content that made someone in the approval chain a little bit nervous.

The Pope's Exorcist campaign had an edge to it. It was bold. It made people do a double-take. It wasn't the kind of content a large agency with a lot to lose would have approved in the first round. But W.L.D.D. was fifteen people with everything to gain and very little to protect, and that freedom was the creative advantage.

If every idea gets smoothed down until it's inoffensive and forgettable, you are not making content. You're making wallpaper. And nobody shares wallpaper.

4. Memes are not a tactic. They are a language.

At W.L.D.D., meme marketing was at the core of how we thought about everything. Not because memes are "trendy", but because memes are how large portions of the internet actually communicate. They are cultural shorthand. They travel fast because they carry meaning efficiently, and they're participatory by nature: people remix them, respond to them, and pass them on.

The brands that treat memes as a tactic, "let's do a meme this week", almost always get it wrong. It feels forced because it is. The brands that understand meme culture as a living, breathing language, and speak it fluently and in real time, are the ones whose content actually gets shared by real people to other real people.

The Pope's Exorcist campaign spoke that language. It didn't just use a meme format. It understood the emotional grammar of how Indian internet culture reacts to horror, to cinema, to the unexpected. That's the difference between speaking a language and just knowing a few words.

5. Speed matters more than perfection.

The internet moves fast. Cultural moments have a half-life measured in hours, sometimes minutes. One of the biggest advantages of being at a small, scrappy startup like early W.L.D.D. was that we could go from idea to execution in a fraction of the time a larger organisation would need. We could see a moment happening, make something about it, and be live before anyone else had finished their first internal approval email.

This is a structural problem for large brands, not a creative one. The people with the best ideas are usually close to the culture. They're young, they're online, they feel the moment in real time. But those people are often several layers away from anyone who can say yes fast enough for the moment to still be alive. By the time the idea gets approved, the moment has passed.

If you're building a brand or running a marketing team, the most valuable thing you can do is create a short approval path for real-time content. Trust the people who are closest to the culture. Give them room to move quickly. A good idea published in time beats a perfect idea published too late, every single time.

Virality is not a formula. It's not a checklist. It's not a budget line item. It's what happens when the right idea meets the right audience at the right moment, and someone was brave enough to make it without waiting for permission.

What happened after.

The campaign changed W.L.D.D.'s trajectory. It changed mine too. It confirmed something I had always believed: that the principles of great filmmaking and the principles of great marketing are the same. Know your audience. Build to an emotion. Be specific. Be brave. Move fast.

I eventually left W.L.D.D., at the peak of my time there, which I'd recommend to anyone, to pursue filmmaking full time in New York. I graduated Dean's List from the New York Film Academy, won at film festivals as both a director and a cinematographer, and founded Thava Studio, a creative studio working with brands across New York, Los Angeles, Philadelphia and Bangalore.

Everything I build now, whether it's a social media strategy for a restaurant in Brooklyn, a short film shot on New York rooftops, or a content system for a luxury brand, runs on the same instincts I developed in that first W.L.D.D. office. The audience is always the film. The brand is always the character. The moment is always the script.

If you're building something right now and trying to figure out how to make it travel, stop asking how to go viral. Start asking what your audience actually feels, what cultural moment you're speaking into, and whether you're brave enough to say something worth sharing. The rest tends to follow.
